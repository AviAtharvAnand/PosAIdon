# EarthEcho Water Challenge — Water Quality Parameters

**Source:** EarthEcho International, *EEI_WC_Parameters (1).docx*

**Scope:** Natural water bodies. The EarthEcho Water Challenge tests described here are not intended for drinking, municipal, or filtered water.

---

## Overview of the EarthEcho Water Challenge

The EarthEcho Water Challenge is a signature program that equips young leaders around the world to become community scientists, testing their local water quality, sharing their data through our global database, and using this data to inform actions to protect local waterways. Using EarthEcho Water Challenge kits (or something similar) people around the world can test their local waterways for four parameters and enter their data into mWater, an accessible global database. Often called citizen or community science, volunteering time to test water can help identify issues and/or establish water quality trends over time. All testing is done in natural bodies of water (our tests are not for drinking, municipal, or filtered water). The four parameters tested using the EarthEcho Water Challenge Kits are water temperature, dissolved oxygen, turbidity, and pH. Each of these parameters help give an overall idea of the health of the ecosystem and the waterbody.

## The Four Water Quality Parameters Tested and Their Importance

### Temperature

Water temperature expresses how warm or cold water is and is defined as the amount of average kinetic energy in water molecules – usually measured in degrees Fahrenheit (F) or Celcius (C). Water temperature is affected by climate, air temperature, weather, and other environmental factors. Water temperature influences many physical, biological, chemical, and ecosystem processes in aquatic environments.

Changes in water temperature (especially increased water temperature) can affect the amount of dissolved oxygen available to plants, animals, and other aquatic organisms, the amount of solubility of metals and toxins in the water, the rate at which algal blooms appear, and more.

More information on water temperature and its effects on ecosystem and water health can be found here: <https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2021-07/parameter-factsheet_temperature.pdf>

### Dissolved Oxygen

Dissolved oxygen is the amount of oxygen available to aquatic organisms. Oxygen in the water is essential for fish spawning, growth, and activity as well as plant and other organisms’ health. Dissolved oxygen level can be a great indicator of overall biological health of a waterbody. Dissolved oxygen can change in a waterbody for several reasons such as seasonal changes to air and water temperature, algal blooms photosynthesizing oxygen at a higher rate than normal, increase in salinity, and more. Usually, the lower the dissolved oxygen rate of a waterbody, the more dangerous conditions become for aquatic life.

Dissolved oxygen is measured in parts per million (ppm) and generally a reading of 3ppm or lower represents low-oxygen levels. Levels between 3-5ppm are often stressful and can still lead to organism death after 12-24 hours. Levels of 6 to 7ppm usually support spawning and growth. Levels of 8+ppm support abundant populations of aquatic life. The EarthEcho kit provides a visual chart for determining dissolved oxygen that has measurements in increments of 0, 4, and 8ppm but participants can make an assessment of any number between 0 and 8.

Decreases in dissolved oxygen can lead to hypoxic (low oxygen) conditions, meaning aquatic organisms are not getting the oxygen they need to survive. Many oxygen-sensitive organisms may die at this point. Continued decrease in dissolved oxygen could then lead to anoxic (no oxygen) conditions which leads to large die offs of fish and other creatures inhabiting the water.

More information on dissolved oxygen in water can be found here: <https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2021-07/parameter-factsheet_do.pdf>

### Turbidity

Turbidity is the measure of water clarity and higher turbidity makes water appear more cloudy, murky, or muddy. Turbidity and total suspended solids are two different ways to measure the same characteristic of water quality. Generally, high total suspended solids and high turbidity go hand in hand. Increase in turbidity can occur due to increase in soil particles, algae, and microscopic organisms. Changes in turbidity can also occur when water temperature is higher and dissolved oxygen is lower.

Turbidity can be measured in several units including Jackson Turbidity Units (JTU) and Nephelometric Turbidity Units (NTU). EarthEcho kits use JTUs. These are a unit of visual measurement and therefore can be subject to personal bias (due to visual impairment, difference of opinion, etc.) but can give a general sense of overall turbidity. Turbidity is measured using a visual cue (in EarthEcho kits this is a black and white sticker at the bottom of the testing cup) and a certain volume of water. The person conducting the test will rate the visual clarity of the sticker and then use a chart provided to match that clarity to a number on the JTU scale. Visual cues are indicated for turbidity measurements of 0, 40, and 100 JTU (with higher numbers indicating higher turbidity) but participants can make an assessment of any number between 0 and 100.

An increase in turbidity can negatively affect aquatic health by clogging fish gills, hindering visibility in the water, decreasing the ability of light to penetrate deeper water which inhibits the growth of plants, reducing aquatic life resistance to disease, and more.

More information on water turbidity can be found here: <https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2021-07/parameter-factsheet_turbidity.pdf>

### pH

pH is the measure of the concentration of hydrogen ions in the water, or more simply put, it is used to determine the acidity of water. pH can be an indicator of chemical, physical, and biological changes in a waterbody and often plays a critical role in chemical processes. pH is measured on a scale from 0 to 14, with 7 being neutral. Water with a pH below 7 is considered acidic and water with a pH above 7 is considered basic. Rain usually has an average pH between 5 and 5.5 while surface ocean water usually has an average pH of 8. pH is a key factor in measuring water toxicity and chemical composition. The EarthEcho kit provides a visual chart for determining pH that has a range of 4-10.

Changes in pH can impact aquatic life by affecting biochemical processes, the metabolism of aquatic organisms, can lead to damage of gills, fins, exoskeleton, and other critical body parts of fish, and can even affect the viability of fish eggs and whether or not they hatch. Many organisms have a preferred pH range, meaning some prefer more basic water (such as snails and clams) while others can survive in more acidic water (such as trout and frogs).

More information on pH and water acidity can be found here: <https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2021-07/parameter-factsheet_ph.pdf>
