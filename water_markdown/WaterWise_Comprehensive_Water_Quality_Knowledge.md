## 1. What does “water quality” mean?

Water quality describes how suitable water is for a **particular use or ecological function**, based on physical, chemical, and biological characteristics. Water suitable for one purpose may not be suitable for another. For example, aquatic-life protection, swimming, drinking, irrigation, and fish consumption require different types of evidence and may be governed by different criteria.

A single number—such as pH or turbidity—cannot establish whether a water body is healthy or safe. Ecological assessments consider several indicators together, including biological communities, habitat, chemical exposures, and changing conditions over time. The EPA National Aquatic Resource Surveys (NARS) use physical, chemical, and biological indicators rather than relying on a single metric.

**Key distinctions:**
- **Observation:** An instrument or laboratory measurement at a given time and place.
- **Indicator:** A measurement or combination of measurements that provides evidence about environmental condition.
- **Contaminant:** A substance present in water that may be undesirable or harmful, depending on concentration and exposure.
- **Pollution:** Contamination or changes that degrade environmental quality.
- **Ecological stressor:** A condition or substance capable of impairing organisms or habitats.
- **Regulatory criterion:** A value or condition recommended or adopted to protect a designated use; it is not interchangeable across uses or jurisdictions.

**Sources:** [USGS Water Quality](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/water-quality); [EPA NARS Indicators](https://www.epa.gov/national-aquatic-resource-surveys/indicators-used-national-aquatic-resource-surveys); [EPA Water Quality Criteria](https://www.epa.gov/wqc/basic-information-water-quality-criteria).

## 2. The four common field measurements

### 2.1 Water temperature

**Definition:** Water temperature is the thermal state of the water, usually recorded in degrees Celsius (°C) or Fahrenheit (°F).

**Why it matters:**
- Influences biochemical reaction rates, metabolism, growth, reproduction, and the distribution of aquatic organisms.
- Warmer water generally holds less oxygen at saturation, although actual dissolved oxygen also depends on photosynthesis, respiration, turbulence, salinity, and other factors.
- Temperature shifts may favor some organisms while stressing others; acceptable conditions are species- and life-stage-dependent.
- Temperature can affect chemical speciation and toxicity for certain pollutants.

**Interpretation safeguards:**
- Compare temperatures at similar seasons, times of day, and depths.
- Do not infer climate-driven warming from a few irregular samples.
- Convert Fahrenheit to Celsius using **°C = (°F − 32) × 5/9**, but retain the original unit and reading.
- Water temperature and air temperature are not the same quantity.

**Source:** [EPA parameter factsheets](https://www.epa.gov/awma/factsheets-water-quality-parameters); [USGS Water Quality](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/water-quality).

### 2.2 Dissolved oxygen (DO)

**Definition:** Dissolved oxygen is the amount of molecular oxygen available in water, commonly reported as milligrams per liter (mg/L), parts per million (ppm), or percentage saturation.

**Why it matters:** Fish and many aquatic invertebrates require oxygen for respiration. Low dissolved oxygen can stress sensitive organisms, restrict habitat, or contribute to mortality. DO can be supplied through air–water exchange and photosynthesis and consumed by respiration and decomposition.

**Broad freshwater screening guidance—not a universal standard:** EPA NARS explains that oxygen below approximately **5 mg/L** is generally stressful to fish; values below **3 mg/L** are generally too low to support fish. Actual impacts depend on species, life stage, duration, temperature, salinity, and local criteria. Some organisms tolerate conditions that others cannot. Do not label all measurements below a cutoff as confirmed fish deaths.

**Why DO varies:**
- Photosynthesis can raise DO during daylight, while nighttime respiration can lower it.
- Warm water has lower oxygen saturation capacity than cold water.
- Decomposition of organic material, including dead algae, consumes oxygen.
- Flow and turbulence promote air–water oxygen exchange.
- Lakes and estuaries may stratify, isolating low-oxygen bottom waters.

**DO measurement advice:**
- Record units, instrument or test method, sampling date/time, depth, and temperature.
- A single afternoon reading can miss an early-morning oxygen minimum.
- Percent saturation is **not** equivalent to mg/L and should not be converted without relevant temperature, salinity, and pressure data.
- In dilute freshwater, ppm and mg/L may be numerically close, but do not blindly merge them without checking the measurement conventions and water density.
- Simple visual kits may produce rounded results, including clusters near specific color-chart values; do not assume decimals imply laboratory-grade precision.

**Sources:** [EPA Dissolved Oxygen Indicator](https://www.epa.gov/national-aquatic-resource-surveys/indicators-dissolved-oxygen); [EPA DO Factsheet](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2021-07/parameter-factsheet_do.pdf); [USGS Dissolved Oxygen](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/dissolved-oxygen-and-water).

### 2.3 pH

**Definition:** pH describes the acidity or basicity of water on a logarithmic scale. A pH of 7 is neutral at standard reference conditions; values below 7 are acidic and values above 7 are basic. A difference of one pH unit corresponds to a tenfold difference in hydrogen-ion activity.

**Why it matters:**
- Organisms have different preferred pH ranges.
- Large or rapid changes can affect physiological functioning and reproduction.
- pH alters the chemical form, bioavailability, and toxicity of some substances. For example, the toxicity of ammonia to aquatic organisms is strongly influenced by pH and temperature.
- Photosynthesis, respiration, buffering, and geological conditions may create natural variability.

**Interpretation safeguards:**
- There is no single universally healthy pH value for every water body or species.
- A pH reading alone does not prove or exclude heavy metals, PFAS, pharmaceuticals, pathogens, or other chemical contamination.
- pH is logarithmic: do not interpret a change from 6 to 7 as a simple linear increase of one unit in acidity.
- Consider natural geology and alkalinity before assuming an unusual pH is caused by pollution.

**Sources:** [USGS Water Quality](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/water-quality); [EPA Ammonia Aquatic Life Criteria](https://www.epa.gov/wqc/aquatic-life-criteria-ammonia); [EPA Water Quality Factsheets](https://www.epa.gov/awma/factsheets-water-quality-parameters).

### 2.4 Turbidity and water clarity

**Definition:** Turbidity describes how suspended matter scatters or obscures light, making water appear cloudy. Common causes include sediment, plankton, algae, and suspended organic material.

**Why it matters:**
- Increased light attenuation can limit underwater plant photosynthesis.
- Suspended material may affect feeding behavior and gill function for some species.
- Runoff events may increase suspended sediment; some environments are naturally turbid.
- Turbidity can be associated with pollutants attached to particles, but it does **not** directly measure PFAS, pesticides, pathogens, or microplastics.

**Units and methods:**
- **NTU:** Nephelometric Turbidity Units, generally based on optical measurement.
- **JTU:** Jackson Turbidity Units; visual and instrument methods differ. They must not be assumed numerically interchangeable with NTU.
- **Centimeters of visibility:** A clarity-depth or tube observation; greater visibility generally indicates clearer water, but it is not a turbidity concentration.
- **Total suspended solids (TSS):** A separate laboratory measurement, commonly mg/L; TSS and turbidity can be correlated but are not identical.

**Interpretation safeguards:**
- Only compare readings using compatible methods and units.
- A storm-related spike is not equivalent to a persistent long-term degradation trend.
- Natural rivers may have changing turbidity during high-flow events even in the absence of a specific chemical spill.

**Sources:** [EPA Water Quality Factsheets](https://www.epa.gov/awma/factsheets-water-quality-parameters); [USGS Water Quality](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/water-quality).

## 3. Other chemical and physical indicators

### 3.1 Electrical conductivity, dissolved solids, and salinity

Conductivity measures how readily water carries electrical current and is influenced by dissolved ions. Specific conductance is commonly normalized to a reference temperature (often 25°C). Salinity is the amount of dissolved salts, especially important in estuaries and marine waters. Total dissolved solids (TDS) describes material dissolved in water and is often inferred approximately from conductivity, but the relationship depends on ionic composition.

Elevated conductivity may reflect geology, seawater intrusion, road salt, wastewater, or other inputs. **Conductivity is not a direct test for a specific toxic pollutant.** Different species have different salinity tolerances, and rapid shifts may be stressful.

**Source:** [USGS Water Quality](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/water-quality); [USGS Field Manual](https://www.usgs.gov/mission-areas/water-resources/science/national-field-manual-collection-water-quality-data-nfm).

### 3.2 Nitrogen and phosphorus

Nitrogen and phosphorus are essential nutrients, but excessive amounts can contribute to algal blooms and eutrophication. Nutrient inputs may come from fertilizers, wastewater, animal waste, stormwater, and other sources. When algae decompose, oxygen consumption may lead to low-DO conditions, especially in lakes, bays, and estuaries.

**Measurement distinctions matter:**
- Nitrate, nitrite, ammonia/ammonium, total nitrogen, orthophosphate, and total phosphorus are not interchangeable analytes.
- Some reports express nitrate as **NO3−**, others as **nitrogen (NO3–N)**; those values require appropriate molecular-weight conversion if scientifically comparable.
- Orthophosphate expressed as **PO4** is not numerically identical to phosphorus expressed as **P**.
- Nutrient concentration alone does not demonstrate a harmful algal bloom or identify its source.

**Source:** [EPA Nutrient Pollution Basics](https://www.epa.gov/nutrientpollution/basic-information-nutrient-pollution).

### 3.3 Ammonia

Ammonia occurs naturally and may also enter water from fertilizer, waste, and wastewater. It can be directly toxic to aquatic organisms, particularly at elevated concentrations. Toxicity depends strongly on **pH and temperature** as well as organism sensitivity. Do not use one fixed universal ammonia threshold without the correct criteria and chemical reporting form.

**Source:** [EPA Aquatic Life Criteria for Ammonia](https://www.epa.gov/wqc/aquatic-life-criteria-ammonia).

### 3.4 Biological oxygen demand (BOD) and organic loading

Biological oxygen demand estimates oxygen consumed by microbial decomposition under defined testing conditions. High BOD can indicate substantial biodegradable organic loading and possible pressure on dissolved oxygen. BOD is not a direct count of pathogens and does not identify the pollution source by itself.

**Source:** [USGS Water Quality Topics](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/water-quality-information-topic).

## 4. Pollutants and emerging contaminants

### 4.1 PFAS

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are a broad family of synthetic substances. Some are persistent, and some may bioaccumulate or pose toxicity concerns. Environmental measurements may refer to individual chemicals such as PFOS or PFOA, not an undifferentiated “PFAS concentration.”

**When interpreting PFAS data:**
1. Identify the exact compound and chemical name.
2. Specify sample medium (surface water, sediment, tissue, treated water, etc.).
3. Preserve units (often ng/L in water); never confuse ng/L with µg/L or mg/L.
4. Distinguish positive detections from non-detects and check reporting limits.
5. Use the relevant **aquatic-life** criterion or tissue criterion where available, rather than substituting a drinking-water criterion.
6. Never infer that PFAS are absent because pH, DO, or turbidity appear normal.

EPA published freshwater aquatic-life criteria for PFOS in 2024, including water-based and tissue-based approaches. A water concentration cannot automatically be evaluated against a fish-tissue criterion.

**Sources:** [EPA PFOS Aquatic Life Criteria](https://www.epa.gov/wqc/aquatic-life-criteria-perfluorooctane-sulfonate-pfos); [EPA Aquatic Life Criteria Table](https://www.epa.gov/wqc/national-recommended-water-quality-criteria-aquatic-life-criteria-table).

### 4.2 Microplastics

Microplastics are small plastic particles or fragments found in aquatic environments. They vary by particle size, shape, polymer, and analytical method. Sources may include fragmentation of larger plastics and releases from manufactured products or fibers.

**Data interpretation safeguards:**
- Sample type (ocean surface, subsurface, beach, sediment), concentration unit, collection method, size-detection limit, and laboratory identification method are essential.
- Ocean-water particle concentrations (e.g. particles/m³) must not be pooled with sediment concentrations (e.g. particles/kg dry weight).
- A higher reported concentration from one study does not necessarily establish a more polluted area if methods differ substantially.
- A measured particle count is not automatically a quantified toxicity or wildlife harm estimate.

NOAA's marine microplastics archive permits geographic and temporal analysis, but NOAA explicitly warns that analytical methods and reporting protocols differ across studies.

**Sources:** [NOAA Marine Microplastics](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/microplastics); [NOAA Microplastics Help](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/microplastics/help).

### 4.3 Pharmaceuticals, pesticides, metals, and other chemicals

Pharmaceutical residues, pesticides, metals, and industrial chemicals can appear in surface waters. Their ecological effects depend on the compound, concentration, exposure duration, life stage, species, chemical form, and environmental conditions. A general water-quality measurement is not a replacement for targeted laboratory testing.

For metals, distinguish dissolved from total recoverable concentrations and account for applicable water chemistry where criteria require it. For pesticides and pharmaceuticals, avoid treating the mere presence of a chemical as proof of injury; evaluate relevant benchmarks and exposure context.

**Sources:** [USGS Water Quality Topics](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/water-quality-information-topic); [EPA Aquatic Life Criteria Table](https://www.epa.gov/wqc/national-recommended-water-quality-criteria-aquatic-life-criteria-table).

### 4.4 Pathogens and microbial indicators

Fecal indicator organisms such as *E. coli* and enterococci can help assess fecal contamination and recreational water risks. An indicator-bacteria count does not directly measure every human pathogen. EPA recreational criteria use statistical approaches over a specified monitoring interval, not just a universal yes/no interpretation of one sample. Aquatic-life and recreational-health assessments address different endpoints.

**Source:** [EPA Recreational Water Quality Criteria](https://www.epa.gov/wqc/recreational-water-quality-criteria-and-methods).

## 5. Biological health indicators

### 5.1 Macroinvertebrates

Benthic macroinvertebrates such as aquatic insect larvae, crustaceans, and mollusks can be indicators of stream condition. Community composition often reflects environmental pressures integrated over time. Some taxa are relatively sensitive to degraded habitat and water quality, while others tolerate a wider range of conditions.

**Limitations:** Counts depend on season, region, habitat, collection gear, and identification quality. A biological index calibrated for one region may not be valid in a different ecoregion.

### 5.2 Fish and aquatic plant communities

Fish assemblages, submerged plants, and algae provide additional evidence of biological condition. Temperature, flow regime, habitat connectivity, vegetation, substrate, nutrient supply, and contaminant exposure may all influence community composition. A poor condition score is rarely attributable to one measured parameter without additional evidence.

### 5.3 Why biology and chemistry should be considered together

A snapshot of pH, turbidity, and DO describes conditions at sampling time; biological communities may reflect longer-standing effects. Conversely, missing or reduced sensitive species can be caused by habitat alteration or flow changes even when routine chemical readings look acceptable.

**Source:** [EPA NARS Indicators](https://www.epa.gov/national-aquatic-resource-surveys/indicators-used-national-aquatic-resource-surveys); [EPA Environmental Indicators](https://www.epa.gov/water-research/environmental-indicators-aquatic-surveys).

## 6. Understanding time trends and monitoring data

**A trend is not just the difference between the earliest and latest observation.** A sound trend assessment needs repeat measurements, comparable methods, adequate time coverage, and attention to seasonality and other confounders.

### 6.1 Recommended metadata fields

For each observation, preserve where available:
- `site_id`, `site_name`, `latitude`, `longitude`, `waterbody_type`
- `sample_datetime`, `timezone`, `sample_depth_m`
- `parameter`, `result_value`, `result_unit`
- `sampling_method`, `instrument`, `laboratory_method`
- `detection_flag`, `detection_limit`, `qualifier`
- `source_dataset`, `source_record_id`, `quality_flags`

### 6.2 Minimum exploratory checks before describing a trend

1. **Location identity:** Are repeated measurements truly from the same location and waterbody?
2. **Unit consistency:** Are numeric values measured using compatible scales?
3. **Calendar coverage:** Which years and months are represented?
4. **Sampling frequency:** Were there multi-month or multi-year gaps?
5. **Sample count:** How many distinct sampling dates support each annual or seasonal estimate?
6. **Timing and depth:** Were measurements taken at comparable times of day and depths?
7. **Data quality:** Are extreme values, repeated exact values, impossible dates, or duplicated events present?
8. **Method changes:** Did observers, instruments, detection limits, or protocols change?

### 6.3 Useful calculations

- **Count and coverage:** observation count, distinct sampling days, number of years, longest gap.
- **Typical condition:** median, mean (where appropriate), interquartile range, minimum, maximum.
- **Seasonal comparison:** compare similar months or seasons across years.
- **Annual summaries:** year-by-year median with count labels and uncertainty where estimable.
- **Trend direction:** calculate a regression slope or robust Theil–Sen slope on a scientifically appropriate time basis; optionally assess monotonic trend with Kendall's tau.
- **Threshold screening:** proportion of observed sampling events below or above a defensible, explicitly named threshold. Report the denominator and whether events represent similar times/seasons.

### 6.4 What not to conclude

- Two observations five years apart do not establish a five-year trend.
- A statistically significant association does not prove causation.
- A non-significant result does not prove no environmental change.
- A high fraction of low oxygen observations may reflect targeted sampling or morning measurements; do not infer the fraction of all hours spent below the threshold.
- Do not equate low DO with detection of a particular pollutant.
- Do not infer pollutant absence from missing results or measurements below a laboratory reporting limit.
- Do not compare national trends using raw cross-site averages without checking whether the sampled-site composition changed.

**Source:** [EPA Data Considerations](https://www.epa.gov/n-steps-online/data-considerations); [USGS Field Manual](https://www.usgs.gov/mission-areas/water-resources/science/national-field-manual-collection-water-quality-data-nfm).

## 7. Joining data across datasets and sites

If a citizen-science record and an EPA/USGS/NOAA observation have coordinates, they may be geographically matched. **Proximity alone does not establish that the records represent the same water or environmental exposure.**

Recommended match labels:
- **Verified same-site measurement:** Same station or confirmed identical sampling site with compatible timing and methods.
- **Nearby same-waterbody context:** Close geographically and on the same confirmed river, lake, bay, or estuary, but not identical sampling events.
- **Regional context:** Same watershed or coastal region but cannot be treated as a paired measurement.
- **Unmatched:** No defensible match.

Distance and date windows are screening tools, **not scientific evidence of an exact match**. In streams, upstream/downstream positioning, tributaries, barriers, and flow direction matter. For coastal samples, tides, depth, and marine setting matter. PFAS water samples should not be blindly combined with fish tissue or sediment values; NOAA ocean-surface microplastics should not be numerically combined with beach or sediment measurements.

**Sources:** [EPA Data Considerations](https://www.epa.gov/n-steps-online/data-considerations); [NOAA Microplastics Help](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/microplastics/help).

## 8. Practical interpretation examples for a chatbot

### Q: What does a dissolved oxygen reading of 4 mg/L mean?

**Suggested answer:** A DO reading of 4 mg/L falls within a range that may stress some fish and other oxygen-sensitive organisms. It warrants context: species present, temperature, time of day, season, measurement method, and how long oxygen remained low. It does **not** prove that fish died or that the entire water body remained below 4 mg/L.

### Q: The pH is 7.2. Is the water safe to drink?

**Suggested answer:** pH 7.2 is slightly basic relative to neutral, but pH alone cannot determine drinking-water safety. Pathogens, metals, organic contaminants, and other hazards require separate testing and applicable drinking-water standards. A community-science surface-water test should not be used to certify potability.

### Q: Turbidity increased after a storm. Does that mean chemicals spilled into the river?

**Suggested answer:** Rain and runoff can raise turbidity by suspending sediment and organic material. A chemical release is one possible concern in some contexts, but turbidity by itself cannot identify a contaminant or pollution source. Consider rainfall, streamflow, site history, and targeted testing.

### Q: Is higher temperature causing lower oxygen?

**Suggested answer:** Warmer water typically has less capacity to hold dissolved oxygen at saturation, and temperature affects biological oxygen demand. But measured DO reflects several simultaneous processes. Plot comparable observations, consider time of day and season, and avoid making a causal claim from correlation alone.

### Q: One site has 20 readings below 5 mg/L; another has 2. Which is worse?

**Suggested answer:** We need sampling denominators, seasonal timing, methods, and site ecology. Twenty low readings out of 200 observations may imply a different monitoring picture from two out of three. Neither count alone proves a more damaged ecosystem.

### Q: No PFAS measurements are recorded for this river. Does it have no PFAS?

**Suggested answer:** No. The absence of PFAS test records indicates that no usable measurements were found in this dataset and search scope; it is not evidence of absence. Even a reported laboratory non-detect means the compound was not detected at the method's reporting capability, not necessarily that its concentration was zero.

### Q: What should a community monitoring group do after finding unusual values?

**Suggested answer:** Verify the original result and unit, repeat sampling where appropriate, document date/time/weather/method, compare with the site's own historical and seasonal range, and consult local environmental agencies or qualified experts if concerning conditions persist. Do not claim contaminant identity or regulatory violations without appropriate tests and standards.

## 9. Rules for the WaterWise AI answer generator

These rules are editorial guidance for chatbot behavior, not quotations from EPA/USGS/NOAA:

1. Explain whether a statement is a **measured observation**, **statistical result**, **scientifically plausible mechanism**, or **hypothesis**.
2. For any site-specific claim, give the site, parameter, units, date range, sample count, and source.
3. Use computed dataset values for numerical claims; do **not** invent site statistics or infer them solely from retrieved explanatory documents.
4. Never declare water safe to drink or swim in based on pH, DO, temperature, or turbidity alone.
5. Never claim ecological injury, a fish kill, or a pollution source from a threshold crossing alone.
6. Separate **EPA recommended criteria**, **adopted state/Tribal standards**, **screening thresholds**, and **educational examples**.
7. Do not mix units, media, or sampling methods merely because their labels sound similar.
8. If sample dates or quality are inadequate for trend analysis, state that a reliable trend cannot be established.
9. Show how a conclusion could be checked: repeat measurement, seek comparable data, investigate method notes, or request targeted contaminant testing.
10. Preserve citations to the source material and distinguish real observations from synthetic demonstration data.
